# Cold outreach knowledge base

The writing principles behind this skill. Read it before writing any message.

Based on Za-zu's cold email handbook (https://za-zu.com/docs/handbook/cold-email/intro), rewritten and adapted for single signal-led messages.

---

## Core principles

### Write one-to-one, even at volume

Cold outreach can go to many people, but each message must read as if it went to one. The recipient should feel they are the only person who could have received it. Anything that reads like a template gets ignored.

### Why cold messages go unanswered

1. **Poor targeting.** The recipient doesn't have the problem, or doesn't have it now.
2. **Poor deliverability.** Badly targeted sends damage sender reputation, so later messages miss the inbox.
3. **Poor writing.** The message talks about the sender, or takes too long to show value.

### Email structure

Keep each section to one or two sentences.

1. **Hook.** Open on the signal: a question, a pain, or a specific observation.
2. **Why.** Show you care about what they care about, and connect it to what you do.
3. **Objection handle.** Answer the one objection most likely to stop a reply. Skip it if nothing obvious stands in the way.
4. **CTA.** One action, answerable with yes or no.

## Tone

### Write to a peer

- Write as you would to a respected peer, not a stranger.
- Drop corporate phrasing and formal openers.
- Keep it warm and brief. Aim between too casual and too formal.
- Use contractions: you're, don't, let's.
- Use the recipient's own industry terms, and only where you're sure they are right.
- No emojis.

### Confident but tentative

Tentative language tends to earn more replies than confident claims, because it invites a conversation.

- Hedge claims: "That likely means…" rather than "This means…".
- Use conditionals: "If I'm reading this right…", "If it's on your list this quarter…".
- Ask rather than tell: pose a hypothesis the recipient can confirm or correct.
- Admit what you don't know about their situation.

---

## Target by pain before persona

A job title tells you who could buy. A signal tells you who has the problem now.

- **Persona targeting:** "Marketing directors at tech companies with 500+ staff."
- **Pain targeting:** "Companies whose job posts show they are rebuilding their website."

Useful ways to spot pain:

1. **Contract timing.** Reach companies shortly before a competitor renewal.
2. **Job changes.** Past users who move to a new company bring their trust with them.
3. **Job posts.** Requirements in a listing reveal priorities and gaps.

`signals.md` lists 52 signals in this spirit.

## Making a claim hold up

When the message makes a claim, it should stand up to a sceptical reader. Borrow the structure of a debate case:

1. **Claim.** What you're suggesting.
2. **Warrant.** Why it follows.
3. **Evidence.** A fact that backs it, from the sender's social proof or the signal context only.
4. **Impact.** Why it matters to this person.

In a short email these compress into one or two sentences. If you can't supply evidence, soften the claim into a question.

## Green flags and red flags

### Green flags

- A specific observation about the recipient's situation.
- An idea tailored to their company rather than their category.
- A timely, specific compliment (a launch, a hire, a post), used only when it's genuine.
- Terminology that fits their industry and role.

### Red flags

- Wrong industry terms.
- Inaccurate facts or faulty inferences from the signal.
- Fake personalisation: a first line that pretends to know more than the signal shows.
- Generic networking requests.
- Anything that feels mass-sent.

## Example emails

These examples are illustrative. Companies, people, and numbers are invented.

### Example 1: champion changed jobs

SUBJECT: Your first 90 days at Northfield

Hi Priya,

Saw you've moved to Northfield to run revenue ops. Congrats.

When you were at Brightline, your team cut forecast prep from a day to an hour with us. Northfield's job posts suggest they still do it in spreadsheets.

No pressure to change anything in month one. Would it help if I sent over the setup you used last time?

[Your Name]

### Example 2: posted a relevant job

SUBJECT: The coordinator role at Harbour Dental

Hi Tom,

Noticed Harbour Dental is hiring a practice coordinator, and the listing leans heavily on "managing the phones".

It seems like missed calls might be part of the reason for the hire. We answer overflow calls for clinics and send a written summary, which some practices use to cover the gap while they recruit.

Would that be useful while the role is open?

[Your Name]

### Example 3: left a negative competitor review

SUBJECT: Your review of the reporting export

Hi Sam,

Read your review about exports breaking whenever a column changes. That sounds painful at month end.

Our exports map columns by name rather than position, so changes don't break them. Moving over takes about a day.

Worth a 15-minute look next week?

[Your Name]

## Key takeaways

1. Make the recipient feel the message was written only for them.
2. Show you understand the pain before you mention the product.
3. Keep each section to one or two sentences.
4. Sound like a peer rather than a vendor.
5. End with one ask they can answer with yes or no.
6. Prefer "Would this be useful?" over "This will help you".
7. Name the signal that prompted you to write.

## LinkedIn guidelines

### Connect notes (under 300 characters)

- One hook only: the single best reason to connect.
- No pitch. Establish relevance and stop.
- Soft ask, such as "Would be good to connect."

**Structure:** personal hook, brief relevance, soft connect ask.

Example patterns:

- "Hi [Name], saw your post on [topic]. The point about [detail] matched what we see too. Would be good to connect."
- "[Name], noticed [Company] is [signal]. I work on [space] and would like to follow along. Happy to connect?"

### Messages (under 500 characters)

- One value proposition only.
- Reference the signal so it's clear you did the homework.
- Write like a short message to a colleague.
- End with one easy question.

**Structure:** signal reference, quick relevance, one question.

Example patterns:

- "Hi [Name], saw [Company] just [signal]. We've helped similar teams [benefit]. Is [specific problem] on your list at the moment?"
- "[Name], noticed you're [signal]. Curious whether [pain] is coming up. We've been working on [brief solution]."
