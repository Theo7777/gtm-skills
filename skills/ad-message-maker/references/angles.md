# The three angles

Each variation takes one angle and only one. Do not blend. The point of three frames with the same photo and layout is that the only thing changing is the message, so the result tells you which message works.

## Angle definitions

**01 Pain.** Name the loss. What it costs them right now, in their words. Wasted evenings, lost customers, a fine for a late return, money left on the table. Make it specific and visceral. The headline should make the right reader wince.

**02 Desire.** Paint the after-state. What does the better day look like once the problem is gone? Quiet evenings, a full calendar, a finished job with nothing waiting. Positive, concrete, no product features yet.

**03 Proof.** Lead with evidence. A number, a result, a customer line, a comparison that lands. "One booking paid for a year." "4.8 stars from 2,000 reviews." "Set up in four minutes." If the only proof is soft, use the most concrete true thing available and flag it in the report.

## On-canvas copy

- **Headline**: max eight words. It carries the angle. Written as a statement or a fragment, never a question with an obvious answer.
- **Pills**: two or three, max three words each. Pills say what the product does, in parallel structure. Same pills can repeat across variations if they are the true top three benefits. Change them only when the angle calls for it.
- **CTA line**: the offer, three to five words. "Download free." "Start your free trial." "Book a 15 minute call." Same across all three unless the offer differs.
- **Brand lockup**: the brand name as text or the logo. Never a tagline on canvas; the pills already do that job.

## Paired platform copy

Three fields per variation. They divide the labour:

| Field | Job | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Hook line | Why should I care? Emotion. Never explains the product. | under 60 chars |
| Explainer | What is this? Must make sense of the product on its own. | under 120 chars |
| Support line | Why should I trust it or act now? Proof or objection answered. | under 255 chars |

Plus one "Testing:" line stating the single hypothesis.

These limits sit inside every major feed placement. Show the character count next to every line so overruns are caught at a glance.

## Voice

- Write like a sharp friend, not a software company.
- Contractions are fine: you're, it's, won't.
- Plain words. "Sorted" over "optimised". "Does your books" over "handles your financial administration".
- Short sentences. Fragments are fine.
- Use the audience's own words for the problem. If the context folder has customer quotes, steal from them.
- Generic framing by default. Do not name a niche unless the user asked for that segment.

## Banned moves

- Corporate language: leverage, streamline, optimise your workflow, boost productivity, next-level, unlock.
- Vague qualifiers: powerful, seamless, revolutionary, game-changing, effortless.
- SaaS phrasing: platform, solution, stack, ecosystem, drive ROI.
- Fortune-500 proof: "trusted by global brands", "enterprise-grade".
- "Helps you to", "enables you to", "allows you to". Use the direct verb.
- Em dashes. Use en dashes or a full stop.
- Emojis, unless the user asks. Then at most one.
- Exclamation marks.
- Questions as headlines ("Tired of doing your books?").
- Promises the product cannot keep.

## Audit before presenting

For each of the three:

- [ ] Headline max eight words and carries one angle only
- [ ] Pills two or three, max three words each, parallel structure
- [ ] Hook under 60, explainer under 120, support under 255
- [ ] Someone reading only the explainer knows what the product is
- [ ] Reads like a friend, not a vendor
- [ ] Could not be confused with the other two
- [ ] No banned words
- [ ] No claim that is not backed by the context or the user
- [ ] British English, en dashes only

## Worked example

A hypothetical product, for illustration only. Product: a receipt-scanning bookkeeping app for UK sole traders. Offer: free 30-day trial. Proof: users spend about 10 minutes a month on their books.

| # | Angle | Headline | Pills | CTA line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | Pain | Your Sunday, gone. Again. On receipts. | Snap every receipt, Sorts your expenses, Ready for tax | Start your free trial |
| 02 | Desire | Books done before the kettle boils. | Snap every receipt, Sorts your expenses, Ready for tax | Start your free trial |
| 03 | Proof | Ten minutes a month. That's the whole job. | Snap every receipt, Ready for tax, Cancel anytime | Start your free trial |

**01 Pain**
Hook: Another Sunday lost to a shoebox of receipts. (45)
Explainer: A bookkeeping app that reads your receipts and sorts your expenses for your tax return. (87)
Support: Snap the receipt when you pay. It files itself. No spreadsheet, no shoebox, no Sunday night panic. (98)
Testing: Whether lost-weekend framing beats the tax-deadline framing.

**02 Desire**
Hook: Books done before the kettle boils. (35)
Explainer: A bookkeeping app that turns receipt photos into tidy accounts, ready for your tax return. (90)
Support: Snap it, forget it. Your expenses sort themselves and your numbers are ready when you need them. (96)
Testing: Whether the calm, finished-books outcome beats loss aversion.

**03 Proof**
Hook: Ten minutes a month on your books. (34)
Explainer: A receipt-scanning bookkeeping app for sole traders. Snap, sort, and send to your accountant. (93)
Support: Users say they spend about ten minutes a month on it. Free for 30 days. Cancel anytime. (87) Needs checking before use.
Testing: Whether a concrete time number converts better than emotional copy.
